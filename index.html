Abstract

From single-step revision to verifiable improvement loops

Recent advances in agentic systems allow AI systems to conduct experiments, interpret feedback, and modify their own workflows, transforming Recursive Self-Improvement from a theoretical concept into an engineering problem. Yet the field still lacks a unified account of what constitutes RSI and how its improvement loop should be evaluated.

This survey defines RSI boundaries, introduces a common Proposal -> Feedback -> Optimization framework, organizes methods across four evolution targets, and identifies major research gaps around evidence-based acceptance, reuse across cycles, and transfer beyond the tasks or evaluators that produced the gains.

Definition

A common loop for modern RSI systems

RSI is treated as an inherited improvement process: a retained change must alter a later improvement cycle and increase the expected quality of subsequent improvements.

01 Proposal

Select an editable target and generate a candidate change through prompting, search, evolution, program synthesis, training, or experimentation.

  • Target selection identifies what the loop is allowed to change.
  • Candidate generation creates concrete artifacts such as prompts, tools, memory, code, data, or checkpoints.
02 Feedback

Expose consequences in a repository, simulator, benchmark, training run, or live environment, then convert outcomes into evidence.

  • Execution environments reveal behavioral effects of a candidate.
  • Verification signals judge the evidence through tests, rewards, model judges, formal checks, or review.
03 Optimization

Retain, revise, reject, promote, or roll back the candidate while preserving memory, provenance, versioning, and validation evidence.

  • Update policies decide whether an apparent gain should persist.
  • Memory and lineage determine whether later cycles can reuse or audit the change.

Future Directions

The RSI maturity test

A stronger RSI claim begins with acceptance, reuse, and transfer: an accepted change must be credible, usable in later cycles, and helpful beyond the evaluator that admitted it.

ReliabilityDoes the change improve the intended capability, not just the measured score?

False positives can be written into memory, skills, or release pipelines; evaluations should report exploit resistance, calibration, trajectory integrity, and held-out transfer.

EfficiencyCan the loop improve within finite compute, time, and review budgets?

RSI needs cost frontiers: gains should be measured against compute, wall-clock time, failed attempts, deployment cost, and review burden.

DiversityDo proposals cover meaningfully different hypotheses and execution paths?

Diversity is about causal independence, not just different wording; systems should preserve disagreement, branch distance, and failure coverage.

PersistenceDoes a successful intervention remain available and useful later?

Retained artifacts need provenance, scope conditions, retirement criteria, and evidence that they contribute downstream rather than drifting into noise.

AdaptabilityCan the system respond to changing tasks, tools, evaluators, and environments?

The key split is frozen evidence for comparison versus adaptive evidence for discovering new weaknesses as the environment changes.

ControllabilityAre authority, review, monitoring, and rollback coupled to risk?

Update authority should track target scope, verifier strength, deployment context, and failure severity, with intervention points preserved across the loop.

GeneralizationDoes the improvement transfer beyond its original task and evaluator?

The strongest claim is transfer of the improvement procedure itself: later generations should search, evaluate, or optimize better because of what was retained.
